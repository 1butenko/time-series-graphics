Time series graphics

Week 2

iWhat you will learn

By the end of this page you should be able to:

  1. choose an appropriate time-series graphic for a question;
  2. distinguish trend, seasonal and cyclic patterns;
  3. use seasonal plots and seasonal subseries plots to see seasonality;
  4. read scatterplots, lag plots and autocorrelation plots;
  5. use the white-noise ACF confidence band to judge whether an autocorrelation pattern is compatible with white noise, and understand how this supports later model-checking discussions.

1 Why time-series graphics matter#

A time series is not just a column of numbers: the order matters. A good graph can reveal patterns, unusual observations, changes over time, and relationships between variables (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.). In this course, those same graphics also prepare us for later tasks such as fitting models and communicating forecasts. In practice, the graph often tells you what question to ask next: Is there a trend? Is the pattern seasonal? Is an apparent relationship linear? Are the autocorrelations compatible with white noise?

Figure 1 is the roadmap for the lecture: start with the time axis, then add graphics that answer more specific questions.

2 Start with the time plot#

A time plot puts the observation value on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. The reading uses time plots as the first graphical view of a series and says that plots help identify patterns, unusual observations, changes over time, and relationships between variables (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.).

2.1 What to look for#

Use the first time plot to scan for three broad patterns (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.):

PatternWhat it means in wordsVisual clue
TrendA long-term increase or decrease; it need not be linear.The baseline drifts upward or downward.
SeasonalA regular pattern at fixed, known intervals such as yearly, monthly, weekly or daily.Peaks and troughs repeat at a known period.
CyclicRises and falls with no fixed frequency, often lasting two or more years and often tied to economic conditions.Waves are visible, but their length changes.

Table 1: Three time-series patterns named in the reading

!Seasonal is not the same as cyclic

A seasonal pattern has a fixed, known period. A cyclic pattern has rises and falls but no fixed frequency (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.).

2.2 Interactive explorer: build a time plot (our example)#

The next figure is an illustration, not data from the reading. Move the controls and watch how the same time plot can show trend, seasonality, both, or neither.

In Figure 2, a non-zero trend tilts the long-run level; a larger seasonal amplitude makes the period-12 wave clearer; extra non-seasonal movement makes that period-12 pattern harder to see.

Example 1 (First read of a time plot (our example)) Suppose the time plot in Figure 2 uses trend strength $0.25$, seasonal amplitude $4$, and irregular movement $1.2$.

Step 1. Check long-run direction. The baseline rises as $t$ increases, so the example contains an upward trend.

Step 2. Check repetition. The wave repeats about every $12$ observations, so the example contains seasonality with period $12$.

Step 3. Check extra movement. Points do not sit exactly on the period-12 wave, so there is additional non-seasonal movement too.

Check. If you set seasonal amplitude to $0$, the period-12 wave disappears while the trend remains. That confirms the two features are different.

iCheck yourself
  1. If monthly data have a repeating annual pattern, what seasonal period would you expect?
  2. If a series rises and falls in waves of unequal length, should you first call that seasonal or cyclic?
iAnswers
  1. $12$, because there are 12 months in a year.
  2. Cyclic, because the frequency is not fixed. If the period becomes fixed and known, then it is seasonal.

3 Choose the seasonal scale#

Seasonality requires a time unit and a period. The reading lists common seasonal periods for different data frequencies (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.). A few entries are enough to build intuition:

Data frequencyNatural seasonal periodExample interpretation
Quarterly$4$ per yearQ1, Q2, Q3, Q4 repeat each year.
Monthly$12$ per yearJanuary through December repeat each year.
Weekly$52$ per yearWeek-of-year pattern.
Daily$7$ per week or $365.25$ per yearDay-of-week or day-of-year pattern.
Hourly$24$ per day or $168$ per weekHour-of-day or hour-of-week pattern.

Table 2: Common seasonal periods from the reading

A single series can have more than one plausible seasonal scale. For example, daily data may be explored with a weekly period ($7$) or a yearly period ($365.25$), and hourly data may be explored with daily ($24$), weekly ($168$), or yearly ($8766$) periods (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.). The reading’s “multiple seasonal periods” examples are a reminder to choose the period from the question, not only from the data frequency.

3.1 Seasonal plots#

A seasonal plot plots the data against time within each seasonal period. It is useful for identifying seasonal patterns and anomalies, and can be used for multiple seasonal periods (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.).

Figure 3 shows the key move: keep the values, but change the horizontal axis from absolute time to within-season time.

3.2 Seasonal subseries plots#

A seasonal subseries plot collects the data for each season together as separate time series. The reading says this makes the underlying seasonal pattern clear and lets changes in seasonality over time be visualized (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.).

PlotHorizontal comparisonBest question
Time plotObservation timeWhat happens overall?
Seasonal plotPosition within the seasonDo years or cycles have the same within-season shape?
Seasonal subseries plotTime within one named seasonIs January changing differently from July?

Table 3: Time plot, seasonal plot and seasonal subseries plot compared

Example 2 (Choosing a seasonal graphic (our example)) You have quarterly tourism data and suspect Q3 behaves differently from the other quarters.

Step 1. Identify the period. Quarterly data have period $4$ per year, from Table 2.

Step 2. Start with a time plot. This checks the overall trend and unusual observations.

Step 3. Add a seasonal plot. Put Q1–Q4 on the x-axis and draw one line per year to see the repeated quarterly shape.

Step 4. Add a seasonal subseries plot. Collect all Q3 values together to see whether Q3 is changing over time.

Check. The seasonal plot answers “within a year, which quarter is high?” The subseries plot answers “within a quarter, is the level changing across years?”

✓Try it

For hourly data, which plot would you use first to compare Monday 09:00 with Tuesday 09:00 patterns: a simple time plot, a seasonal plot with a daily period, or a seasonal subseries plot with an hour-of-week grouping?

iSolution

Use a seasonal subseries-style grouping by hour of week if the goal is to compare named positions such as Monday 09:00 and Tuesday 09:00 over time. A daily seasonal plot can reveal hour-of-day shape, but it mixes weekdays unless you also separate by day.

4 Relationships between variables: scatterplots and correlation#

When there is more than one variable, the reading introduces scatterplots, correlation, and scatterplot matrices (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.). A scatterplot puts one variable on each axis; a scatterplot matrix repeats this idea for several pairs of variables.

The reading defines correlation as a measure of the extent of a linear relationship between two variables (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.). For paired observations $(x_t,y_t)$, the sample correlation is

\[ r=\frac{\sum\left(x_t-\bar{x}\right)\left(y_t-\bar{y}\right)}{\sqrt{\sum\left(x_t-\bar{x}\right)^2} \sqrt{\sum\left(y_t-\bar{y}\right)^2}}. \tag{1} \]

Here $x_t$ and $y_t$ are the paired values at time $t$, while $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ are their sample means.

!Correlation sees linear association

The reading illustrates that correlation measures linear relationship; therefore a scatterplot is still needed to see shape, outliers, and non-linear structure (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.).

Example 3 (Calculating a small correlation (our example)) Take three paired observations: $(x,y)=(1,2),(2,4),(3,5)$.

Step 1. Compute means. $\bar{x}=2$ and $\bar{y}=\frac{11}{3}$.

Step 2. Compute centered values. For $x$: $-1,0,1$. For $y$: $-\frac{5}{3},\frac{1}{3},\frac{4}{3}$.

Step 3. Use Equation 1.

\[ \begin{aligned} r &=\frac{(-1)(-5/3)+0(1/3)+1(4/3)}{\sqrt{(-1)^2+0^2+1^2}\sqrt{(-5/3)^2+(1/3)^2+(4/3)^2}} \\ &=\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{42/9}} \approx 0.982. \end{aligned} \]

Check. The value is close to $1$, matching the nearly straight increasing pattern of the three points.

iCheck yourself

If two variables have $r\approx 0$, what should you do before concluding there is “no relationship”?

iAnswer

Look at the scatterplot. A near-zero correlation rules out strong linear association, but the plot may show a curved pattern or unusual observations.

5 Dependence over time: lag plots and autocorrelation#

A time series can be related to its own past. The reading introduces lag plots: each graph shows $y_t$ against $y_{t-k}$ for lag $k$ (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.). The autocorrelations are the correlations associated with these lag plots; for example, $r_1$ is the correlation between $y_t$ and $y_{t-1}$, and $r_2$ is the correlation between $y_t$ and $y_{t-2}$ (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.).

For a series $y_1,\ldots,y_T$, the reading gives the autocorrelation at lag $k$ as

\[ r_k=\frac{\sum_{i\in\{k+1,\ldots,T\}}\left(y_i-\bar{y}\right)\left(y_{i-k}-\bar{y}\right)}{\sum_{i\in\{1,\ldots,T\}}\left(y_i-\bar{y}\right)^2}, \tag{2} \]

where $\bar{y}$ is the sample mean and $T$ is the length of the series (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.).

5.1 How to read an ACF plot#

The reading gives three key rules (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.):

  1. when the data have trend, autocorrelations for small lags tend to be large and positive;
  2. when the data are seasonal, autocorrelations tend to be large and positive at seasonal lags, such as $12$ and $24$ for monthly data with yearly seasonality;
  3. when the data are both trended and seasonal, autocorrelations tend to be large and positive at small lags and at seasonal lags.

Example 4 (Reading an ACF pattern (our example)) Suppose monthly data have large positive ACF bars at lags $1$, $2$, $12$, and $24$.

Step 1. Interpret small lags. Large positive values at $1$ and $2$ suggest nearby observations are similar, which is consistent with trend.

Step 2. Interpret seasonal lags. Large positive values at $12$ and $24$ match annual seasonality in monthly data.

Step 3. Combine the evidence. The ACF pattern is consistent with a trended and seasonal series.

Check. The seasonal period is $12$, so the repeated ACF peaks at $12$ and $24$ are exactly where Table 2 tells us to look.

✓Try it

Weekly data show strong positive autocorrelation at lags $52$ and $104$. What pattern does this suggest?

iSolution

It suggests yearly seasonality in weekly data, because the seasonal period is about $52$ weeks, and $104$ is two such periods.

6 White noise and the ACF confidence band#

The reading shows white noise and its ACF, then states that for white-noise data the sampling distribution of $r_k$ is asymptotically $N(0,1/T)$ (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.). Therefore an approximate 95% confidence interval for $r_k$ is

\[ \pm \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{T}}, \tag{3} \]

where $T$ is the length of the series (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.).

Syllabus bridge. The reading-supported fact is the white-noise ACF distribution and band in Equation 3. When later lectures ask whether a fitted model has left time dependence behind, this is the graphical ingredient to remember: an ACF pattern compatible with white noise should have most bars inside the approximate band. This page does not add a full residual-diagnostics procedure because the reading does not give one.

Example 5 (ACF band width (our example)) A series has length $T=100$.

Step 1. Substitute into Equation 3.

\[ \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{100}}=\frac{1.96}{10}=0.196. \]

Step 2. Interpret. The approximate 95% white-noise band is from $-0.196$ to $0.196$.

Step 3. Use visually. A lag with $r_k=0.40$ lies outside this band; a lag with $r_k=0.05$ lies inside it.

Check. If $T$ increases, $\sqrt{T}$ increases, so the band becomes narrower.

iCheck yourself

For white noise with $T=400$, what is the approximate 95% ACF band?

iAnswer

$1.96/\sqrt{400}=1.96/20=0.098$, so the band is approximately $[-0.098,0.098]$.

7 Presenting forecasting results starts with the same graphics#

The syllabus asks you to present forecasting results. This reading does not give a separate forecast-display recipe. What it does give is the graphical vocabulary that a later forecast display will reuse: time plots, seasonal periods, anomalies, relationships and ACF checks (“02 - Time Series Graphics.md,” n.d.). The course-level bridge is simple: when communicating future values, keep the historical time scale and the relevant patterns visible, but treat this as an application of the reading’s graphics rather than a forecast rule stated in the reading.

8 Putting it together#

A good workflow is to move from broad to specific: use a time plot first, seasonal graphics when the period matters, scatterplots when variables relate, and lag/ACF graphics when dependence over time matters.

9 Summary#

!What to remember
  • Time plots are the starting point: they reveal patterns, unusual observations and changes over time.
  • Trend is long-term increase or decrease; seasonality repeats at a fixed known period; cyclic movement does not have a fixed frequency.
  • Seasonal plots compare positions within a season; seasonal subseries plots collect each season as its own small time series.
  • Correlation measures linear relationship, so always read it with a scatterplot.
  • Lag plots compare $y_t$ with $y_{t-k}$; the ACF records those correlations across many lags.
  • Trend tends to create large positive ACF at small lags; seasonality tends to create large positive ACF at seasonal lags.
  • For white noise, an approximate 95% ACF band is $\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T}$.
